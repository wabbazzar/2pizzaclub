# Story prompt — *Someone's Eating Babies*

A creepy nursery rhyme for adults. Mother Goose cadence, Donaldson engine, sourced-conspiracy substrate.

## What it is

A short illustrated book in the register of an English nursery rhyme. Eight scenes plus interludes. Every line scans like something a child could chant; every referent ties to a documented, primary-sourced fact about the network this site catalogues. The horror is not invented — it is the public record, set in nursery meter.

The book is *not* a polemic. The narrator does not explain. The reader is shown — exactly as the existing 2pizzaclub timeline does — and decides.

## What it is modeled on

- **Mother Goose** — the AABB couplet, the simple-noun vocabulary, the refrain that returns once and never has to return again to keep ringing.
- **Julia Donaldson, *Room on the Broom*** — the engine: a small object drops at the witness's feet at the end of each scene, and the accumulating burden becomes the salvation in the final scene. Donaldson's *the broom broke* moment is the structural model for the eighth scene; the bog-covered animals are the six (or seven) objects the child gathers.
- **Edward Gorey, Mother Goose's darker register, the *Faustbuch*, Tophet inscriptions, Slavic vampire folklore, Sethian Gnostic theology** — the underneath. The book never says "Gnostic." The book makes the reader feel it.

## Example excerpts — the models

### Mother Goose (public domain)

The cadence + the unflinching dark register. These were sung to children for centuries and never softened. Four canonical examples:

**Solomon Grundy** (first collected by Halliwell, 1842):

> Solomon Grundy,
> Born on a Monday,
> Christened on Tuesday,
> Married on Wednesday,
> Took ill on Thursday,
> Worse on Friday,
> Died on Saturday,
> Buried on Sunday.
> This is the end
> Of Solomon Grundy.

A whole life in ten lines. The foreshortening — birth-to-burial in eight beats — is the move scene 5 of *Someone's Eating Babies* uses to compress centuries of Phoenician sacrifice into a single stanza: *long, long ago by the warm salty sea / they built a small altar of bronze, on the lea.* Compress centuries into a couplet.

**Goosey Goosey Gander**:

> Goosey Goosey Gander,
> Where shall I wander?
> Upstairs, downstairs,
> And in my lady's chamber.
> There I met an old man
> Who wouldn't say his prayers,
> I took him by the left leg,
> And threw him down the stairs.

Concrete settings (chamber, stairs), recognizable historical referent (the priest-in-hiding of the English Recusancy period, *the old man who wouldn't say his prayers* = the Catholic forced underground), violence delivered flat with no editorializing. This is the model for the book's voice. The rhyme does not say *and this was tragic*; it says *I took him by the left leg and threw him down the stairs.*

**Sing a Song of Sixpence**:

> Sing a song of sixpence,
> A pocket full of rye,
> Four and twenty blackbirds
> Baked in a pie.
> When the pie was opened,
> The birds began to sing,
> Oh, wasn't that a dainty dish
> To set before the king?
> The king was in his counting house,
> Counting out his money,
> The queen was in the parlour
> Eating bread and honey.
> The maid was in the garden
> Hanging out the clothes,
> When down came a blackbird
> And bit her on the nose.

Layered tableau (king + queen + maid), tight rhyme, and the closing landing the violence on the working woman, not on the royals. Class is in the structure. The book's parked clintons draft (now in git history) follows the same architecture — president, wife, the laptop, the disposable functionaries who watched it.

**Three Blind Mice**:

> Three blind mice, three blind mice,
> See how they run! See how they run!
> They all ran after the farmer's wife,
> Who cut off their tails with a carving knife,
> Did you ever see such a thing in your life,
> As three blind mice?

A knife. A mutilation. A cheerful refrain. The closest analog for the book's overall register — the horror is recited as if it were quaint, and the quaintness is the indictment.

### Julia Donaldson — *Room on the Broom*

In copyright (Macmillan, 2001). The full text cannot be reproduced here. The engine, however, is described — and the engine is exactly what *Someone's Eating Babies* runs on:

- A protagonist (the witch) acquires a new companion each scene: cat, dog, bird, frog. Each arrival uses the same refrain: a polite request to join the broom.
- Each addition adds weight. Eventually the broom snaps in two. The witch falls into a bog.
- A dragon corners her. The four companions, covered in bog mud, rise up as a single composite creature and frighten the dragon away.
- Resolution: the witch builds a new broom with seats for all five. **The accumulated burden is the salvation.**

That is the structure of scene 8 (*the telling*). The child has gathered six (or seven, if *the small doors* is wired in) small objects, one per scene. At the climax she lays them in row, and the cumulative inventory is what allows her to refuse the entity. Donaldson's *bog-covered animals rising to scare the dragon* is the *child laying the six things in row* moment — the same engine, transposed.

Donaldson also writes in tight AABB couplets, simple Anglo-Saxon vocabulary, anapest-leaning meter, and an exact refrain that returns identically each time it appears. That is the cadence to match. Buy the book — it is the single closest reference text for this project.

Reference: Julia Donaldson + Axel Scheffler, *Room on the Broom*, Macmillan Children's Books, 2001.

## Permission framework — the payoff in scene 8

The final scene is the child refusing the entity. Research across Slavic vampire folklore, Sethian Gnosticism, the *Faustbuch*, the Sinai covenant, the Lesser Key of Solomon, the Tophet inscriptions, and Christian deliverance-ministry literature yielded **four load-bearing conditions** the entity requires before it can enter a soul, plus two secondary ones:

1. **invitation / threshold-crossing** (Slavic vampire folklore, Briggs's fairy scholarship, Derek Prince) → *bow*
2. **oath / covenant sworn** (Sinai → Faust → Goetia → generational-curse theology) → *vow*
3. **offering given at the altar** (Tophet inscriptions, Leviticus, grimoire propitiation) → *offering*
4. **interior assent / forgetting** (Sethian Gnostic Archon theology, Christian deliverance "open doors") → *sigh*
5. *the name spoken* (grimoire tradition, Crowley, Exodus 23:13) → *name*
6. *the mark accepted* (Revelation 13, Crowley sigils, Goetia) → *mark*

The child names what the entity wanted from her — *name, sigh, mark, vow, offering, bow* — and refuses each. The book the reader is holding is her telling.

## Voice rules

- **Direct.** No flamboyance. No rhetorical kickers ("the contrast is the point", "the through-line", "the receipts are below"). No editorializing about meaning. The image carries the freight.
- **AABB couplets, simple nouns.** The cadence is the fairy tale. The referents are the receipts.
- **Real referents, never fairy-tale generics.** Jerusalem — not "a far land." A blue-striped white temple — not "a dark castle." A yacht in the British sea — not "a country of stone." Specific named places, specific named people (first names only in verse: *Danny*; surnames only as needed in the draft annotations). The creepiness emerges from the *contrast between* nursery cadence and historical fact, not from genre-fantasy imagery.
- **The mirror is the device, set in scene 1, never re-invoked verbally.** The illustrations carry it after the opener. Each subsequent scene is what the mirror shows the child; the reader sees this through the art, not through the text.
- **The refrain *Someone's eating babies* appears once, in scene 1. The book trusts the line to echo on its own.**
- **No "we" / "us" / "our"** in editorial framing. Verse may carry *we / our* as the speaker-voice within the rhyme (*we always have done*, *under all of our walls*). This is the inside-the-story voice, not the editorial voice.
- **Character consistency across scenes.** Recurring figures use a fixed epithet across pages: the child is *she*; Robert Maxwell is *the dead man* (set in scene 2, recurs in scene 6); the entity is *the dark thing* (set in scene 5, recurs in scene 7). Same character, same name, every time.
- **Acrostic and other concealed structure are permitted and encouraged.** Scene 6 (the small doors) hides *PROMIS THE OCTOPUS* in the line-initial letters. The reader who notices, notices. The book never points at its own tricks.

## Working rules

These are the working rules. Every draft submitted by an agent must hold all four.

### 1. Cohesion across scenes

The book is one story, not eight standalone rhymes. The engine is the mirror (scene 1) showing the child a continuous unfolding across history; the interlude couplet — *And the mirror went still. And the mirror went grey. / A small [X] lay at her feet at the dawn of the day.* — runs between every scene and accumulates objects at her feet. The accumulation is load-bearing: by scene 8 the child arranges what she has gathered and uses the inventory to refuse the entity. Donaldson's broom-of-friends, transposed.

Recurring characters keep their epithets across pages. *She* is the child (scenes 1, 8). *The dead man* is Robert Maxwell (scenes 2, 6 if *the small doors* is wired in). *The dark thing* is the entity (scenes 5, 7). New scenes that touch existing characters must use the established epithet. Do not call Maxwell *a man* in one scene and *a tinker* in another and *the dead man* in a third. Pick one. Hold it.

When introducing a new scene, audit the existing scenes for any character, place, object, or motif it touches, and make sure the new scene's language is consistent with what the book has already said.

### 2. Mystery grounded in fact

Two failure modes are equally bad:

- **All mystery, no grounding** — fairy-tale nouns floating in air. *Tinkers, lords, kings, swords, countries of stone, dragons.* This was rejected. It is the failure mode the book is built against.
- **All grounding, no mystery** — Wikipedia in couplets. *Robert Maxwell, who died Nov 5, 1991, off the Lady Ghislaine, distributed PROMIS via Mossad.* The receipts are in the source records and the timeline, not in the verse. The verse has to be a fairy tale that *also happens to be* the receipts.

The line lands when the mystery and the grounding coexist: *On a hill in Jerusalem a man went to sleep / in a tomb of pale stone that was windy and steep.* The reader who knows the dossier sees the Mount of Olives. The reader who doesn't sees an image. Both are correct.

### 3. No made-up lines without meaning

**This is the hard rule.** A line that scans and rhymes but does not point at a documented fact is not allowed. Every noun a line introduces — every place, every object, every named or unnamed actor — must answer the question *what real thing is this?* before the line stays in the draft.

If the line invents an object (a *small leather pack* the journalist did not actually carry), invents a place (a *country of stone* that is not any specific country), invents a quote the speaker did not say, or invents an actor (a *tinker* who corresponds to no one in the record), the line is cut.

This is not an aesthetic rule. It is the editorial integrity of the project. The site's argument is *here are the receipts.* Verse that adds invented detail to make the meter scan is verse that contaminates the receipts. Cut the line. Find the real referent. If the real referent will not scan, the scene is wrong, not the rule.

### 4. The agent reviews and explains every line

Before any new scene or revised stanza is submitted, the agent must produce, line by line, a brief note identifying the primary-source referent each image points at — in the format used by the per-stanza annotations in `children_story_1.md`:

> - **L1–2** — [referent], with link or evidence-record id.
> - **L3** — [referent].

Lines without an attached referent note do not get submitted. The annotation is the proof-of-work that rule #3 is being honored — and it is also the artifact that lets the human editor decide whether the compression chosen is the right one. If a line cannot be annotated because no referent exists for it, the line is invented and must be cut or replaced.

The annotation is not optional and is not a documentation chore. It is **how the agent demonstrates the line is honest.**

## Production constraints

- Lives at `eating/index.html`, alongside the timeline.
- No build step. Plain HTML + a small inline `<script>` for hash-routed pagination.
- Each scene is one page (one hash route: `#i`, `#ii`, …). Navigation: left/right keys, on-screen arrows.
- Typography: Caveat Brush + Schoolbell + Quicksand. Background `#ede4d3` (aged paper). No animation beyond a soft fade on page change.
- Illustration is the second-pass workstream. The text is written so that **every image cue is implicit** in the verse: a child + a mirror + a small object on the floor. The illustrator gets the room and the object from the lines and fills the dread.
