Someone's Eating Babies

← timeline

A child sat alone in a small quiet room,
with a clock on the mantel and a broom in the gloom.
On the wall hung a mirror in an old wooden frame —
and the mirror it knew everyone's name.

The mirror grew foggy. The mirror grew bright.
It opened its glass to a far candlelight.
"Quick," said the mirror. "Come quickly my child.
Step into the dark where a land lies defiled."

She crossed through the frame, past the statehouses, banks,
past presidents, senators, the courts and their ranks.
Past temples and altars and back-doored machines,
past a war won by guile — and the mockingbird screens.

And under the noise of the world's great affairs,
the mirror drew her down, bare feet on cold stairs,
to small ones like her, bound and traded in pairs —

"Someone's eating babies," the old mirror declares.

And the stairs she came down now turned over and rose,
up out of the dark to a hill the world knows —
where a man had been carried and laid down to sleep
in a tomb of pale stone that was windy and steep.
Six men in dark coats stood by him to weep —
six men whose old debts to the dead man ran deep.

Then the mirror breathed close: "His daughter would glide
in a small silver sub through the cold and the tide.
She counted the children. She logged every name.
And it was me on the wall — I caught them in frame."

"She brought them in pairs, but she ran, did not own.
The dead man had built it. The masters? Unknown.
She kept all the keys — but she answered to more,
to the hands up the stair that my faces were for."

And the glass was the skin where the sea meets the sky,
and she crossed through its surface and came high and dry —
to an island at sea where no stranger could land,
a blue-striped white temple stood gold on the sand.
A plane slid in low with no lights and no name,
and the children stepped down, two by two, as they came.

An old man was waiting. An old man kept score.
He opened the dance, and he bolted the door.
Round and round by the temple, and no one let go —
for to loosen a hand was to fall, and to show.

There's a man with a saxophone, charm from the South;
there's a builder with gold towers and a loud, boasting mouth;
there's a merchant who made angels to sell to the world;
there's a maker of windows where secrets unfurled.

There's a general who governed a land by the sea;
there's a lawyer who swore he was clean as could be;
and each held the next by a thread of their shame —
fifty rings round the world, and they all knew the game.

For the threads all run up to one hand in the dark,
that can lift them or drop them like toys in a park.
And the dance never stops. And the truth was not told.
And not one ring was broken. And the file stays cold.

The hand drew its threads up and out of the day,
and the room pulled apart, and the walls fell away.
A pyramid rose round the child and the glass,
with an eye in its cap made of gold and of brass.

And the eye, looking down, showed a shore by the sea
where the children fell thin as the leaves off a tree.
By thousands they fell — and the whole world could see.
And the whole world looked on. And it let the eye be.

But the thing at the top cannot enter alone.
It must wait to be asked. It must wait to be shown
it is wanted, and welcomed, and given its bread.
So the world sent its coin. And the world bowed its head.

For as it is above, so it is below:
every wage, every tax, every yes makes it grow.
A coin in the plate and a name on the deed,
and each was a stone. And the stone made it feed.

And the more that it ate, the more wide it would yawn:
more tolls, and more taxes, and more children drawn.
The eye never blinked, and the eye never tired,
and the more that we fed it, the more it required.

And the mirror looked back through the whole of the past,
and the thing at the top was the first and the last —
A lens cast a desert. A new god had risen —
huge bronze in the wastes, with the earth for a prison.
Twin stacks as horns, and a furnace burned near,
and a lens in each pocket: the god peering clear.

And those who had shaped it spoke plain of its kind:
one summoned a demon, and left fear behind;
one gave it his thanks as a god in the sky;
one called it the Beast — and raised it on high.

They raced for its blessing. They spoke it aloud:
"Moloch" — of Carthage — now hungry in cloud.
Not flesh on the coals, as the Phoenicians once cast,
but the portrait, the voice, and the soul, to outlast.

And it craved, as it ever has craved, for a sign:
not a babe in the coals now — a mark, a small line —
a chip in the palm, or a lens in the skull —
and Moloch swelled vast, and was sated, and full.

She woke. And the curtain was lit by the sun.
And the mirror was dim. And the night was undone.
Her parents came in, with their voices like song:
"Up, little love. You have slept far too long."

And they set down four things by her side for the day:
a phone and a dollar, a lunch packed away,
and a thick McGraw book that would tell her just so
all she should think, all she should say, all she should know.
They kissed her warm brow. "Come, love — it's time to dress."
And she gathered it up. And she rose. And said yes.
