Someone's Eating Babies

← timeline

A child sat alone in a small quiet room,
with a clock on the mantel and a broom in the gloom.
On the wall hung a mirror in an old wooden frame —
and the mirror it knew everyone's name.

The mirror grew foggy. The mirror grew bright.
It opened its glass to a far candlelight.
"Quick," said the mirror. "Come quickly my child.
Step into the dark where a land lies beguiled."

She crossed through the frame, past the statehouses, banks,
past presidents, senators, the courts and their ranks.
Past temples and altars and back-doored machines,
past a war won by guile — and the mockingbird screens.

And under the noise of the world's great affairs,
the mirror drew her down, down the back of the stairs,
to the small ones held down, to be traded in pairs —

"Someone's eating babies," the old mirror declares.

And the stairs she came down now turned over and rose,
up out of the dark to a hill the world knows —
where a man had been carried and laid down to sleep
in a tomb of pale stone that was windy and steep.
Six men in dark coats stood by him to weep —
six men whose old debts to the dead man ran deep.

Then the mirror breathed close: "His daughter would glide
in a small silver sub through the cold and the tide.
She counted the children. She logged every name.
And it was me on the wall — I caught them in frame."

"She brought them in pairs, but she ran, did not own.
The dead man had built it. The masters? Unknown.
She kept all the keys — but she answered to more,
to the hands up the stair that my faces were for."

ii. the maxwells

On an island at sea where no stranger could land,
a blue-striped white temple stood gold on the sand.
A plane slid in low with no lights and no name,
and the children stepped down, two by two, as they came.

An old man was waiting. An old man kept score.
He opened the dance, and he bolted the door.
Round and round by the temple, and no one let go —
for to loosen a hand was to fall, and to show.

There's a man with a saxophone, charm from the South;
there's a builder with gold towers and a loud, boasting mouth;
there's a merchant who made angels to sell to the world;
there's a maker of windows where secrets unfurled.

There's a general who governed a land by the sea;
there's a lawyer who swore he was clean as could be;
and each held the next by a thread of their shame —
fifty rings round the world, and they all knew the game.

For the threads all run up to one hand in the dark,
that can lift them or drop them like toys in a park.
And the dance never stops. And the truth was not told.
The temple still stands. And the file stays cold.

iv. the clintons

Up the steps of a townhouse, behind a tall door,
there hung a strange portrait nobody could ignore:
a President smiling in red shoes and blue,
and the plane to the island had flown him there too.

His wife wore a pantsuit. His wife kept the files.
She closed every cabinet and wiped all the trials.
A laptop was found in a smaller man's drawer.
Of twelve men who watched it, nine watched no more.

Together they kept what each one of them knew.
The pantsuit, the closet, the portrait, the crew.
On each other's birthdays: "Hate you. Palm Beach."
And the cameras kept rolling. The truth out of reach.

And the mirror went still. And the mirror went grey.
A small painted portrait lay at her feet at the dawn of the day.

v. the old altar

Long, long ago by the warm salty sea,
they built a small altar of bronze, on the lea.
They called the small thing by a name — they called it 'Baal' —
and they fed it the children, by ones, and by all.

The mothers would weep. The fathers would queue.
The line at the altar was always so new.
The flames would rise high. The small bones would fall.
And the dark thing grew large. And the dark thing grew tall.

The dark thing still lives. The dark thing still feels.
The bronze became glass. The flame became reels.
We bring it our small ones — we always have done.
The mirror grew dim. And the mirror grew gone.

And the mirror went still. And the mirror went grey.
A small charred bone lay at her feet at the dawn of the day.

vi. the new altar

Today in the desert, a great mill spins on.
A city of brass with no morning, no dawn.
Tall stacks above. Pipes coiling below.
And the whining of spindles that no man can slow.

The mill takes small portraits. The mill takes small voices.
The mill takes the heartbeats. The mill takes the choices.
Each name on a punch card. Each face on a plate.
The great brass machine eats them all. It won't wait.

The mill has long fingers — a thousand small hands.
The mill has bright eyes — every face it commands.
A black smoke curls upward. A wheel turns the gear.
And the small thing inside grows large. The dark thing is here.

And the mirror went still. And the mirror went grey.
A small brass chip lay at her feet at the dawn of the day.

vii. the telling

The child gathered her six things and laid them in row —
a shoe and a camera, a key with no glow,
a portrait, a bone, and a brass-bodied chip.
The mirror cracked open. The mirror let slip.

She turned to the glass and she looked it in the eye.
"You wanted my name. You wanted my sigh.
You wanted my mark. You wanted my vow.
You wanted my offering. You wanted my bow."

"I give you no name. I give you no mark.
I give you no oath. I keep my own dark."
The mirror grew small. The mirror grew thin.
She opened her mouth — and you heard it begin.
